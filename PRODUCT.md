# Product

## Register

product

## Users

Independent auto repair shop owners running 1-10 locations. Most are not technical. They're used to clunky, expensive industry software (Mitchell1, Tekmetric, ShopMonkey) or no software at all (whiteboards, spreadsheets, phone calls to managers). They check in on their shops from a laptop or phone, often before their first coffee. Secondary users: service advisors managing the daily RO board, and technicians checking their job queue from a phone between lifts.

## Product Purpose

ShopCommand gives shop owners real-time visibility into every location without being physically present. Repair orders, tech efficiency, revenue, staffing. The landing page exists to convert skeptical shop owners who have been burned by overpriced, overcomplicated software before. Success: a shop owner signs up, connects their shop, and within minutes sees data they used to drive across town to get.

## Brand Personality

Direct, capable, no-BS.

Like a competent shop foreman who earns trust through results, not pitches. Says what it does, does what it says. No buzzwords. No overselling. Respects the owner's time and intelligence. Confident without being flashy.

## Anti-references

- **Generic SaaS templates.** No Framer/Webflow/v0 template energy. No identical card grids. No three-step "how it works" with numbered circles and connector lines. No warm-cream-with-serif-headings trending aesthetic. If someone could guess "AI made this," it failed.
- **Old-school auto industry software.** Not Mitchell1's dated interface. Not the clunky, Windows-XP-era look that shop owners are used to suffering through. ShopCommand should feel modern without feeling foreign.
- **Overdesigned startup aesthetic.** No glassmorphism, no gratuitous gradients, no design-award-chasing over usability. No "this looks impressive but I can't figure out where to click." Shop owners are practical people; the design should be too.

## Design Principles

1. **Show the product, not the pitch.** The dashboard preview and real feature screenshots do more convincing than copy ever will. Lead with what the tool actually looks like.
2. **Respect the audience.** Shop owners detect BS instantly. They deal with vendors all day. Every claim should be specific and verifiable. No vague promises.
3. **Practical over pretty.** Every design decision should make the tool easier to understand or use, not just look better in a portfolio. If a visual choice doesn't serve the user, cut it.
4. **Earn trust through transparency.** Show pricing openly. Admit what's not built yet. Be honest about where competitors are ahead. This audience values straight talk.
5. **Tool energy, not marketing energy.** The landing page should feel like the front door to a capable tool, not a separate marketing site designed by a different team.

## Accessibility & Inclusion

No formal WCAG compliance target, but usability is non-negotiable. Many shop owners are 40-60, may have reduced vision from years of shop work, and are not tech-savvy. Practical requirements: readable text sizes (16px+ body), sufficient contrast, keyboard navigable forms, clear focus states, no information conveyed by color alone. Respect prefers-reduced-motion. Touch targets sized for hands that work with tools all day.
